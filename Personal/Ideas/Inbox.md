## 2026-08-24 — Fertility crisis is incentives, not housing/money
- Spark: X reply to someone blaming housing as boomer passive income for the fertility problem (handed over from Atlas chat)
- The idea (his words): The fertility crisis comes down purely to incentives — there is no incentive to have more kids. Families who are more well off, on average, are also below the threshold. There is no data to prove that money is actually the limiting factor here, as much as it might seem like it at first. You would think not enough money would be the main factor given the state of the economy, but oddly enough it is not. The only groups that do are certain church going folk. The real problem is that kids add no economic value to a family. When on the farm, they were free labor. Then, when we moved into cities and abolished child labor, kids consume until they move out. They only make parents' lives harder, economically speaking, not emotionally.
- Deeper mechanism (his words): What allowed a population boom — for most of human history, parents took care of kids until the kids could take care of themselves, and the kids that were taking care of themselves then in turn took care of their parents. People's retirement strategy WAS kids. Now we have 401Ks and social security for retirement income, the genesis of the 40/40/40 plan, which meant that it wasn't your kids, but other people's kids. That, and when child labor laws were put in place, kids provide no real economic value in the home and are primarily something to be taken care of. When they grow up now, they usually work on striking out on their own and building their skills, but there is no expectation of them providing for their parents in kind, as their parents provided for them when children, because the retirement expectation arrived as a result of what was discussed prior. We have effectively engineered every incentive to not have kids, as they add nothing but take everything, looking again at it from a purely economic lens.
- One deepening note (only if he answered): Church-going folk have more kids more as a cultural reason — a value they deem important — not because there is economic value in doing so. That cultural value itself becomes a kind of incentive, though a weak one. Individuals are unpredictable, groups are very predictable, and money is one of the greatest incentives of all. Given enough time and without enough concentration of energy (purity spirals that know when to stop spiraling, like a thermostat), people tend to veer away from those ideals and fall mostly to economic incentives.
