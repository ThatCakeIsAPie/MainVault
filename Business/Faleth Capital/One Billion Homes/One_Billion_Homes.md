# One Billion Homes

*How the first Faleth subsidiary builds homes, trains the people who build them, and pays them for the homes they finish.*
*Faleth Capital · Subsidiary Framework · Version 1.0 (Draft)*
*Runs under [[The Contribution Framework]], [[The Cell Framework]], [[The Governance Framework]], and [[The Financial Framework]]. The philosophy is in the [[Faleth Capital Constitution]].*

---

## 1. The Name Is the Mission

One Billion Homes exists to be so good at building homes that an ordinary family can afford one, and eventually more than one. The number is a moonshot by design. It isn't a forecast. It's a direction, and a standard every decision gets measured against: does this get us closer to a billion, or not?

Housing is the largest line in most household budgets, and construction productivity has barely moved in decades. A home's cost is mostly the hours of skilled people, the markup at every handoff between companies, and the time a house sits unfinished. All three are problems of how the work is organized, and organizing work is what Faleth was built to do.

We don't win by keeping prices high. A builder that profits from scarcity has every reason to keep supply short. We profit by lowering what it costs to build, and we sell at market. Selling below market only hands the difference to whoever flips the house, so the way prices fall is supply, and the way supply grows is a builder whose costs keep dropping. Builders who can't keep up either raise their standards or leave the market, and their customers become ours. Every home a competitor builds to our standard still counts toward the mission.

**Every home is numbered.** Each one carries a plaque — *Home #412 of 1,000,000,000* — and the running count is public, on the website and on the trucks. The first public milestone is one million. The count is the score, and the crews who built each number can point to it.

## 2. The Home Is the Ticket

Under [[The Contribution Framework#3. The Value Share|the Contribution Framework]], every completed unit of work funds its own labor pool from its own gross margin. Here the unit is a home.

**Cost of goods** is everything bought from outside and built into the house: the lot, the materials, government and utility fees, and any work still done by an outside subcontractor. **Gross margin** is the sale price minus that. The **cost of fulfillment** rate turns gross margin into the home's labor pool, and the points each person earned on that home decide their share.

The reference model makes the stakes concrete. For the average new home in NAHB's 2024 survey, built entirely by our own people:

| | Amount | Share of price |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sale price | $665,298 | 100% |
| Lot | $91,057 | 13.7% |
| Materials | $158,652 | 23.8% |
| Fees | $20,267 | 3.0% |
| **Gross margin** | **$395,322** | **59.4%** |

Today's market pays about $153,000 for the on-site labor and the subcontractors' overhead on that home, roughly 39% of the gross margin. That is the rate the team has to beat. A crew that builds the same home in fewer hours raises the value of every point on it.

**Every trade brought in-house moves money into the pool.** While a trade is subcontracted, its whole price sits in cost of goods and nobody here earns from it. Once our own people do that work, the materials stay in cost of goods and everything else becomes gross margin the team is paid from. Vertical integration isn't only a strategy for the company. It is a raise for everyone in it.

These are the reference model's figures, and they get replaced with the subsidiary's own as homes close.

### Paying before the house closes

A home takes months to build and gets paid for at closing. The team can't wait that long, so each home's labor pool is set when its price is: the contract price for a presold home, the list price for one built for inventory. Points are earned as each stage passes its interface (§3), and they pay weekly against that pool. When the home closes, the pool is trued up to the actual sale price, up or down, in the same pay period.

The subsidiary carries that pay out of working capital until closing. Weekly pay ahead of revenue is a cost of being in this business, and it sits on the books like any other.

### The quality holdback

A small share of each home's pool is held back and released at the home's one-year walkthrough. A home isn't really finished until it has lived through its first year without trouble. If it has, the crews who built it get paid a second time. If it hasn't, the cost of the callbacks comes out of the holdback first ([[#4. The Guarantee|§4]]). The size of the holdback is a team parameter, set at calibration.

## 3. Cells Follow the Build

Under [[The Cell Framework#2. Map the Work, Then Draw the Cells|the Cell Framework]], structure follows the flow of the work. A home moves through a sequence of stages, and each stage becomes a cell once the work outgrows a single team. Each handoff is an interface with a clear standard, so a stage either met it or it didn't.

| Cell | Receives | Delivers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Land and Entitlement | A target market and capital | A buildable lot with approved plans and permits |
| Site and Foundation | A permitted lot | A graded pad and a stressed, inspected slab |
| Component Plant | Plans and a build schedule | Wall panels, trusses, and pre-built assemblies, sequenced for delivery |
| Framing and Set | A slab and a component delivery | A framed, sheathed, inspected structure |
| Envelope | A framed structure | A dried-in house: roof, windows, doors, and exterior finish |
| Systems | A dried-in house | Plumbing, electrical, and HVAC roughed in and passed inspection |
| Interiors | A house with rough-ins passed | A finished interior ready for final inspection |
| Sales and Closing | A finished home, or one under way | A closed sale and a homeowner who knows what they bought |
| Homeowner Care | A closed home | A first year without surprises, and a hand-off to estate care |

Early on, One Billion Homes is one cell doing some of this and buying the rest. As each trade comes in-house, its stage becomes work the team does itself, and eventually a cell of its own.

The subsidiary-layer points split each home's pool among the stages, negotiated by the Leads on each side of every seam ([[The Cell Framework#3. Two Layers of Points|the Cell Framework]]). Inside each cell, the people who do the work point their own processes.

Two things travel with every home through every cell: its number, and its clock.

## 4. The Guarantee

We guarantee our homes for longer than the industry standard, and the guarantee is wired into pay. Three things make it work.

**The reserve is a liability.** At closing, a warranty reserve for each home goes onto the books as a liability. That reduces the subsidiary's book value growth for the quarter, so the gain share already prices the guarantee in. A team that builds well sees the reserve released as homes pass their walkthroughs. A team that builds poorly sees callbacks eat into it.

**Callbacks come out of the holdback first.** The crews who built a home are the first to pay for its mistakes and the first to be paid for getting it right ([[#2. The Home Is the Ticket|§2]]).

**Homeowner Care owns the relationship.** One cell answers the phone for every homeowner during the guarantee, sends the right crew, and records what failed and why. Those records feed back to the cells that built the house, so a mistake gets fixed in the process and not just in the house.

The guarantee is also a sales advantage. A long warranty on a numbered home, backed by crews with their own money riding on it, is a promise most builders can't afford to match.

## 5. The Apprentice Ladder

The trades don't lose young people because the work is hard. They lose them at the bottom of the ladder, where pay is low, nobody is paid to teach, and one broken-down car can end an apprenticeship. One Billion Homes rebuilds those rungs.

**Apprentices are paid for the work, not the hour.** Layer 2 pays apprentices for the processes they complete, at the same point values anyone else gets for them. Masters and journeymen hand off repetitive work so they can focus on what only they can do. On the reference model's electrical line, the labor and subcontractor overhead come to about $8,970 a home. Setting boxes, drilling, and pulling wire take about 80 of its 144 hours. If the team points that work at half the pool, the apprentice who does it earns about $4,485, roughly **$56 an hour**. At a third of the pool it's still about $37. The master spends the other 64 hours on the panel, the service, and the trim-out, and earns about $70 an hour on the remaining points. Both earn more than the market pays, because the subcontractor's markup is now in the pool.

**The ladder is visible.** Each certification and license unlocks processes an apprentice couldn't legally do before. Their pay grows with their skill, and they can see the next rung from their first week.

**Teaching is paid.** Developing an apprentice is an indirect process with points of its own, earned when the apprentice passes a milestone: a certification, a licensing exam, a stage they can now run alone. Teaching becomes a career step instead of a favor. In the words of [[Faleth Capital Constitution#Article IX: What This Is For|the Constitution]], the job is to equip people until they no longer need you.

**The floor covers the long climb.** An apprenticeship takes years, so the team can give apprentices a longer non-credited window on the floor than the standard one ([[The Contribution Framework#2. The Three Layers|the Contribution Framework]]). The floor is there so that nobody quits the trade over a bad month.

**The tools and the truck belong to the cell.** Individuals don't own assets in this system, cells do ([[The Governance Framework#5. Separation|the Governance Framework]]). A crew's truck, fuel, and tools sit on its cell's books, so a dead car is a cell's maintenance problem instead of an apprentice's crisis. A cell may also choose to fund other supports from its own ledger, such as help with child care or an emergency fund.

**The skills stay because staying pays.** A competitor can offer a trained person a higher wage. It can't offer them gain share, Class B, a guarantee holdback on homes with their name in the count, or a path to running a subsidiary of their own. Class B travels with the person who earned it, so nobody is trapped ([[Faleth Capital Constitution#Article V: Separation and the Peaceful Fork|Article V]]), and the reason to stay is simply that it pays better than leaving.

## 6. Speed

The goal is a home built from start to finish in under a month. Then under a week. Then under a day.

Anyone can grow homes per month by hiring more people. Fewer can shorten days per home, and shortening it frees up the people and the capital for the next home sooner. So the subsidiary tracks two numbers and publishes both every quarter alongside its financials:

- **Days per home**, from permit to closing. The Census average for a single-family home is 8.8 months.
- **Homes per month**, set by whichever stage has the least capacity.

The slowest stage sets the pace for everything, so improvement goes there first. When a stage looks balanced and nothing stands out, the team sets a target the current process can't meet and finds the next bottleneck that way. A step that looks fully optimized at 30 days is full of waste at 7.

Speed pays the people who create it. Points are earned on completion, not by the hour, so a crew that builds the same stage in half the time earns the same pool twice as often. When the team improves a process, the points stay on it until the next calibration, flowing mostly to whoever made the improvement, and then the gain spreads to everyone ([[The Contribution Framework#5. Efficiency and Adaptation|the Contribution Framework]]).

## 7. Bringing Work In-House

Work comes in-house in order of where it pays the most: the trades with the widest gap between installed cost and materials, the most labor, and the most time on the schedule. On the reference model, that starts with framing, where the installed cost is about 6.4 times the lumber, followed by plumbing and electrical rough-ins, the slab, and flatwork.

The **Component Plant** is how framing and the trades get faster. Wall panels, trusses, pre-wired assemblies, and pre-built plumbing trees turn weeks of skilled site work into days of assembly. Published studies have measured component framing at about half the labor hours of stick framing, or less, and the savings land on our books because we own the crews and the plant.

The plant sells to other builders too. Whatever our own homes can't use yet goes to other builders, Habitat for Humanity affiliates among them, which keeps the line running at full speed and lowers the cost of every unit. A home built by a competitor with our panels and our trained crews still counts toward the mission. Plant sales are business-to-business, so the referral program doesn't apply to them ([[The Marketing and Brand Framework#2. The Referral Program|the Marketing and Brand Framework]]).

## 8. Where It Meets the Old Rules

The industry has decades of regulation built around wages and subcontracting. We work inside it.

**Licensed trades.** Texas licenses electrical, plumbing, and HVAC work and sets rules for how apprentices are supervised. Licensed masters and journeymen are built into the Systems cell's points, and supervising an apprentice is a pointed process like any other.

**Federally funded work.** Federally funded construction requires prevailing wages. For that work, the floor is set non-credited at the prevailing rate for the duration of the job, and Layer 2 earnings above it pay as usual.

**Hours.** Construction workers are non-exempt, so the 40-hour cap and Time on Task apply in full ([[The Financial Framework#7. Hours, Overtime, and Time on Task|the Financial Framework]]).

**The guarantee and the law.** Texas courts recognize implied warranties of good workmanship and habitability on new homes. Our written guarantee goes beyond them, and the reserve covers both.

Employment counsel sets the exact classification and payroll mechanics before the first offer goes out.

## 9. The Hand-Off to Estate Care

Every home One Billion Homes builds comes with a period of free care from Faleth's estate care subsidiary. One Billion Homes buys that care at estate care's normal price and books it as a cost of acquisition, the same line as any incentive to buy a home. Each subsidiary keeps honest books, and the homeowner gets a household that runs itself from the day they move in.

The hand-off also protects the guarantee. Estate care crews visit every home on a schedule, and anything they notice — a slow leak, a sticking door, a crack — goes straight to Homeowner Care before it becomes a callback.

Because One Billion Homes builds whole blocks at a time, its homes give estate care its densest routes. A crew that serves a whole street spends its day working instead of driving.

The cell boundaries, the cost of fulfillment rate, the stage point values, the holdback, the apprentice floor, and the warranty terms are parameters the people doing the work set and revisit each quarter. The commitments are the parts meant to last: every home is numbered, every home is guaranteed, the people who build it are paid for building it and paid again when it holds up, and the measure of the company is how many families it puts in a home of their own.*
