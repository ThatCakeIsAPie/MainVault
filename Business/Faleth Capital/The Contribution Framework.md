# The Contribution Framework

*How pay works — a process-based model that replaces wages with value, ownership, and freedom.*
*Faleth Capital · Internal Framework · Version 2.0*
*Companion to [[The Governance Framework]], [[The Cell Framework]], and [[The Financial Framework]]. Ownership and equity rest on the [[Faleth Capital Constitution]] and [[The Equity Framework]].*

---

## 1. Why Not Wages

Wages and salaries sever the link between contribution and reward. An hourly wage punishes efficiency: the faster you work, the less you earn. A salary punishes ambition: the more you do, the more you subsidize the people who do less. Both quietly cap effort, and both treat human labor as a commodity bought by the hour or the year — an arrangement that assumes people won't work unless they're supervised into it. We start from the opposite assumption.

Before the modern economy, people worked in small groups where the tie between effort and reward was obvious. When a hunting party brought down a mammoth, everyone ate. The fastest runner spotted the herd, the strongest threw the spear, the wisest led — and nobody assigned those roles from above; people did what they were best at because the outcome mattered to all of them equally. There was no free-rider problem, because contribution was visible. There was no ceiling on effort, because more effort meant more food for everyone, yourself included.

This framework recreates that inside a modern business. The pool is collective, the share is earned, and the better the business does, the better everyone does. Three commitments hold it together. We **pay for value created, not time spent** — no clock, so the same result earns the same whether it took you one hour or four, and efficiency is rewarded instead of punished. We organize around **processes, not roles** — the work that needs doing, not a set of job titles, so people flow to where they add the most. And **anyone can calculate their own pay** — the formula is open and checkable to the penny, with no black boxes and no bonuses decided behind a closed door.

This is not socialism, though a shared pool invites the guess. Splitting the reward equally, regardless of who created it, breaks the tie between what you produce and what you earn — the same flattening that wages and salaries grew out of, approached from the other side. Both cap effort; both assume people must be compelled rather than trusted. We do the reverse: the pool is shared, but each person's share is proportional to the value they create, never an equal ration handed down. This isn't a retreat from free enterprise — it's free enterprise carried all the way to the people doing the work.

That everyone shares in the upside and the downside — that each person is, in a real sense, an owner — is the ground the whole system rests on, and it belongs to the [[Faleth Capital Constitution]]. What this document defines is the machinery that makes it real: how the pay actually works.

> Pay for the value, not the hours it took.

## 2. The Three Layers

Pay has to do three different jobs at once: keep you steady enough to think past this week, reward what you actually produce, and tie you to the long-term health of the whole. One layer handles each, on its own clock.

**Layer 1 — the floor.** A guaranteed weekly minimum beneath which no one's take-home falls. It isn't a wage and it isn't charity; it's what removes survival anxiety, because people running on fear don't think long-term, take smart risks, or help each other. The floor is deliberately modest — enough to cover basic living in the local market, no more — because its whole job is to let the other two layers work. The team sets it and indexes it to local cost of living. When a slow week drops someone below it, the gap is carried as a credit against future earnings — a soft advance that clears itself as things recover, capped at a few weeks' worth so it never snowballs. New participants get more grace than that. To start, their floor isn't credited at all: however far into a quarter someone joins, they get the rest of that quarter plus the entire next one as an outright grant, never an advance to repay. Through that window the Leads watch a little more closely and make sure they get the help to climb — the aim is that by the time the floor starts counting against future earnings, they're already comfortably above it. Anyone using this as supplemental income can opt out of the floor and simply earn what they produce; the one condition is that they say so, since the floor also protects the team's trust that everyone is carrying their end.

**Layer 2 — the value share.** The weekly engine, and where most pay comes from: each person earns a share of the value they personally helped create, unit by unit. It's the heart of the model, and the next section is how it's calculated.

**Layer 3 — the ownership stake.** The floor keeps you steady and the value share pays you for today; Layer 3 makes tomorrow yours. It exists for one reason: the cleanest feedback loop for work that helps the business is a share of the profit that work produces, so that when the company benefits, you benefit — directly, in your own pocket. Each quarter, a share of profit is split among the team by how much each person earned in Layer 2 — some tied to their own team's results, some to the whole portfolio's — and any of it can be taken as cash or turned into ownership. The math of those profit shares lives in [[The Financial Framework]], and how ownership is minted and valued lives in [[The Equity Framework]]; what matters here is the logic — a growing slice of your pay is tied to the enterprise you're helping build. That is what turns a job into ownership.

## 3. The Value Share

Layer 2 is calculated per unit of work. Every completed job generates its own small labor pool out of its own gross margin, and each person earns from that job based only on the points they put into it. Your weekly pay is just the sum of those per-job earnings — the specific person paid for the specific value they created on the specific ticket.

The formula is short. A unit's **gross margin** is what the customer paid minus what the materials cost. Multiply that by the **Cost of Fulfillment (COF) rate** — the share of gross margin set aside for labor — and you get the unit's **labor pool**. Your cut of that pool is your points on the job over the total points on the job. That's it, one ticket at a time.

Because the pool comes from each unit's own margin, the dollar value of a point floats. A high-margin job makes every point on it worth more; a thin one makes each worth less. Labor cost scales with the value of the work automatically, and the person who grows a ticket — by adding a service, or catching a second problem the customer didn't know they had — earns directly from the larger pool they created.

Gross margin is the anchor on purpose: it's the value the team makes with its own hands and judgment — the spread between price and parts — and the team moves it every day through the quality, speed, and sourcing of the work.

A quick example. A phone comes in for a $300 screen replacement with $150 in parts, and the shop's COF rate is 30%. Gross margin is $150, so the labor pool on this job is $45. Say the work is worth 7.2 points all told, and one tech earned 6 of them doing the diagnosis and repair: their share is 6 ÷ 7.2 of the $45 pool, or **$37.50**, while whoever handled the check-in and check-out earned the other 1.2 points, or **$7.50**. Add a case and a screen protector that lift the ticket to $360 with $180 in parts, and the pool grows to $54 — the same points now pay more, because the work created more value.

## 4. Points and Processes

Nobody here has a job description. We list every process the business needs — every discrete thing that has to happen for work to get done and money to come in — put a point value on each, and let people decide what to work on. You walk in, look at what needs doing, and ask what you can contribute right now. One person likes the counter, another likes the bench, a third mixes it by the day. The system doesn't care who does the work, only that the work gets done and the contribution is tracked.

Processes come in two kinds. **Direct processes** generate revenue — they take a customer's problem and turn it into completed, paid work: check-in, diagnosis, the repair itself, quality check, check-out. **Indirect processes** protect revenue — the things that keep direct work possible: sourcing parts, managing inventory, handling cash, and the coordination work of the Lead. Both are pointed, because both create value; the difference is only whether the value shows up on the ticket or behind it. Ordinary upkeep — a clean bench, a swept floor, answering the phone like a professional — is more of a standards question than a compensated activity, unless the team decides otherwise.

The Lead is the one real role the system has, and even then it's a set of indirect points rather than a rank. The Lead earns a per-device rate on everything that moves through the shop, whoever does the hands-on work, and it accrues whether or not they're on the floor that day — the same freedom from the clock everyone else has. Those points belong to the elected seat, so they move only by election. Where a team elects more than one Lead, they divide the functions among themselves however fits the work. A Lead can also pick up direct processes and earn direct points like anyone else. How Leads are elected and held accountable is in [[The Governance Framework]].

Points are earned on completion, not by the hour — finish a restoration in 45 minutes or two hours and it pays the same — and they're logged in the open, so anyone can see the running totals, the margin so far, and what a point is currently worth. The values themselves are set by the team and revisited every quarter, which is the calibration process in [[The Governance Framework#3. Calibration|the Governance Framework]].

## 5. Efficiency and Adaptation

A system that pays for time has to fear efficiency. A system that pays for value feeds on it. That difference is the flywheel at the center of this framework: simplify the work and there are fewer total points on it, which makes every remaining point worth more. The person who finds a better way isn't cutting their own pay — they're concentrating it.

The reward starts personal. When someone improves a process so it takes a fraction of the effort it used to, the points on it stay where they are for the rest of the quarter — juicy, and flowing mostly to whoever made the improvement. That's the prize for innovating. At the next calibration the now-redundant points come out, and since the pool divides among fewer points, everyone's remaining points are worth more from then on. The gain begins private and ends shared.

Automation is the same mechanism rather than an exception to it. Points are earned on completion, so someone who uses a new tool to finish a diagnosis in five minutes instead of thirty earns exactly what they always did, just faster and more often. When a tool takes a process over entirely, that process leaves the board at the next calibration and its points dissolve into the value of what remains. No reorg. A conversation between peers about what the work is now.

Suppose work that took a hundred people now takes thirty. In a traditional firm that's a layoff. Here it isn't, because nobody was bound to a role to begin with — the constraint has just moved from supply to demand, from "can we do the work?" to "is there enough work?", and the valuable processes shift with it, toward acquisition and drumming up the demand that refills the pipeline. Either you're doing the work or you're finding more of it, and people flow to whichever side is short.

None of this rides on one brilliant stroke. Winning is rarely a silver bullet; it's a million golden BBs — small improvements made by the people closest to the friction, compounding across the whole business. This framework exists so that those people are the ones who profit from finding them.

---

*This is a living framework. The parameters — point values, the COF rate, the floor amount, how work is divided into processes — are set by the people doing the work and revisited each quarter. The principles are the parts meant to last: pay for value created rather than time spent, organize around processes rather than roles, and keep the math open enough that anyone can check their own pay.*
