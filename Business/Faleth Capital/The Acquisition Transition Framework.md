# The Acquisition Transition Framework

*How an existing business joins the portfolio without coercion, surprise, or broken trust.*
*Faleth Capital · Internal Framework · Version 2.0*
*Companion to [[The Contribution Framework]], [[The Governance Framework]], and [[The Financial Framework]]. The acquisition commitment itself is [[Faleth Capital Constitution#Article VI Acquisitions|Article VI of the Constitution]].*

---

## 1. Why Acquisitions Are Different

Faleth Capital doesn't only build businesses from scratch. When an existing business and the person running it align with the mission, the office can buy it and bring it into the portfolio.

But acquiring isn't founding. A founded subsidiary starts clean — everyone who joined chose to, and the Contribution Framework is the only system that business has ever had. An acquired company has people who didn't choose this. They chose a job, under a boss, with a paycheck their family may depend on, and habits and relationships built around a system that's about to change underneath them.

Handle that the way we handle everything else — voluntary participation, real transparency, a genuine exit for anyone who doesn't want to be here — and what remains is a team that chose this with open eyes. Handle it carelessly and we get exactly what the traditional model produces: compliance without conviction.

What happens to the seller depends on the deal. Buying from another private equity firm or family office means they're paid and gone. An individual who wants to keep running the business — someone who came looking for help, or for a system they believe will make it better — is paid, typically places the proceeds in a Managed Capital Account, and stays on as the acting executive. Between those sits a range of arrangements, all of them terms of a transaction rather than matters of principle.

The principles are what follow. Timelines, sequencing, and specific terms flex with the business. How people are treated does not.

## 2. The Commitments

Five promises to every person inside a business we acquire, identical in every deal.

**You will not be surprised.** Everyone learns what's happening, why, and what it means for them personally before anything changes — in direct conversation, not a memo from new ownership.

**Your compensation is honored.** Existing wages become each person's Minimum Pay Guarantee, non-credited, so no advance balance accrues against future earnings. That holds from the day of acquisition through their department's transition, and then continues on the same terms any new participant gets: the remainder of that quarter plus the whole of the next ([[The Contribution Framework#2. The Three Layers|The Contribution Framework]]). Nobody takes a pay cut on day one, and nobody starts the new system already owing it.

**You'll see it working before you're asked to work in it.** Not a presentation or a video — time spent inside a live subsidiary, watching real people do real work in a system where effort visibly determines income.

**You'll have a real choice with a real exit.** Anyone who decides it isn't for them leaves with severance at their previous rate, and someone from Faleth Capital works with them personally, at our expense, to land them somewhere else. How a person is treated when they leave matters more than how they're treated when they stay. Terms scale with the business; the principle doesn't. Nobody should ever feel financially pressured into staying.

**The transition moves at a pace that respects the business and the people in it.** It happens in stages, around the work rather than instead of it. The business never stops serving its customers, and the departments that go first become proof for the ones still waiting.

These are non-negotiable. Everything else — durations, sequencing, severance terms — is a parameter set at the time of acquisition.

## 3. The Transition

No two acquisitions are alike, and the sequencing, timing, and department order belong to the executive and the office at the time. But every transition follows the same arc.

**Business as usual.** The company keeps operating exactly as it did. No structural changes, no compensation changes, no new systems; existing managers keep their authority, and schedules and reporting lines stay intact. This builds trust, and it buys time to learn how the business actually runs — not how the org chart says it runs, but how work really flows, who makes things happen, and where value is genuinely created. Behind that, the office and the executive are mapping processes, studying the cost structure, and beginning to design the point system and COF rate this subsidiary will eventually run on. None of it is visible yet. It's preparation, not implementation.

**Education and observation.** The Contribution Framework is introduced to the whole team — philosophy, mechanics, and math — with honest answers to the hard questions. Then people are invited, not required, to spend time at a working subsidiary: the dashboards, the point tracking, the weekly payouts, the quarterly profit share, and conversations with people who live in it every day. Documents can explain the system. Only a working subsidiary can prove it.

At the end of that comes the **opt-out window**, where every person makes a real choice: transition, or take the severance and go. This isn't a cost to be minimized. It's what makes the confidence of the team that stays worth anything, because everyone still there chose to be.

**Department by department.** The rollout proceeds one team at a time, typically starting closest to direct value creation, where the link between effort and output is most visible and most immediately rewarding. As each department transitions, existing wages convert to the non-credited floor, processes are mapped and point values calibrated with the team ([[The Governance Framework#3. Calibration|The Governance Framework]]), the COF rate is set, the pool begins operating, and tracking goes live so people can watch their earnings in real time. The first department across becomes the proof for every one that follows.

**Full integration.** Once every department has transitioned, the subsidiary runs on the same terms as any other: the office fee applies and quarterly reporting feeds the consolidated numbers ([[The Financial Framework#1. How Money Moves Through a Subsidiary|The Financial Framework]]), participants gain full access to profit share and Class B minting ([[The Equity Framework]]), the team elects its first Leads ([[The Governance Framework#2. Leadership & Representation|The Governance Framework]]), and the first quarterly calibration is scheduled.

At that point it isn't an acquired business in transition. It's a subsidiary, structurally indistinguishable from one built from the ground up.

## 4. The Middle Management Question

Acquisitions surface something most organizations avoid naming. The Contribution Framework replaces management hierarchy with self-organization, tracked contribution, and elected leadership. For most people that's straightforwardly better. For someone whose career was built on managing others, it asks a harder question: what do I produce?

That isn't a judgment. Plenty of middle managers are excellent coordinators, mentors, process designers, and problem solvers, and those skills translate directly into the Lead role or into high-value indirect processes. Some will find they were always good at the direct work and got promoted away from it, and the framework offers a path back with no stigma attached.

But there's no role for someone whose only function is making sure other people work hard enough. The incentive structure does that now. Anyone in that position deserves to hear it honestly — not on day one when everything feels threatening, but during the observation period, when they can see for themselves what the system does and doesn't need.

Which is why that period matters most for this group. A manager watching a subsidiary run well without traditional management usually has one of two reactions: they see where they fit and get energized by it, or they recognize the system doesn't need what they've been selling and leave with clarity instead of resentment. Both beat clinging to a title that no longer carries authority, slowly souring, and eventually poisoning the culture they were meant to support.

A generous exit for someone whose role has been structurally dissolved isn't charity. It's respect.

*An acquired business is not a conquered territory. It is a group of people being invited into a better system. The invitation has to be genuine, or it isn't an invitation at all.*
