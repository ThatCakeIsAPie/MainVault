# The Financial Framework

*How money moves through the Faleth Capital ecosystem.*
*Faleth Capital · Internal Framework · Version 2.3*
*Companion to [[The Contribution Framework]], [[The Cell Framework]], and [[The Equity Framework]]. The philosophy behind the structure is in the [[Faleth Capital Constitution]].*

---

## 1. How Money Moves Through a Subsidiary

Every layer takes its share from a number it can see, in a fixed order, and nothing is taken twice. The whole financial system is that one sentence repeated at three scales.

Inside a subsidiary the sequence runs like this. **Revenue** comes in, and subtracting the **cost of goods** — the materials and parts consumed — leaves **gross margin**, the value the team created with its own hands. The **cost of fulfillment** comes out of gross margin first. It's the labor pool: weekly pay for every process a person does, from finding the customer and closing the sale to delivering the work. Then the **cost of acquisition** — the outside spend it took to win customers, such as ads, lead services, and referral credits — comes out along with the subsidiary's other operating expenses, amortized across the work rather than dropped in as a lump. What survives is **operating profit**.

Two more things come out after that, and they are calculated side by side rather than one after the other: the office fee and the subsidiary's quarterly gain share. Both are measured against how much the subsidiary's **book value** grew over the quarter, before either is paid, so neither shrinks the base the other is drawn from. What remains is the subsidiary's own **net profit**, kept for reinvestment and reserves.

A subsidiary's book value is counted the way the office counts its own — assets less liabilities, less unrealized gains and losses ([[The Equity Framework#2. Earning and Cashing Out Class B|The Equity Framework]]) — so every layer is measured by the same number. Most of a quarter's growth is operating profit. What a subsidiary buys stays on its books as an asset, so a purchase changes the form of its value rather than the amount.

The **office fee** is one-third of that growth, so a subsidiary contributes in proportion to how well it did, and nothing in a quarter it didn't grow.

A fifth of it goes to the people who serve the office and its investors: 14% to the subsidiary's executive team and 6% to the mentor who developed them ([[The Governance Framework#2.1 — The Executive Seat|The Governance Framework]]). The other 80% goes to the pool's liquid side, owned by the office and its investors in the same proportions as everything else ([[#2. The Office Pool|§2]]).

The fee is the whole of what the office charges a subsidiary. Overhead is never allocated downward, so a subsidiary's margin is set by its own spending and nothing else.

The fee works the same way one layer further in. A cell is a subsidiary under a subsidiary, with a ledger inside the subsidiary's books instead of a bank account of its own, and a multi-cell subsidiary charges each cell one-third of the growth in the cell's book value ([[The Cell Framework#4. One Body, One Pool|The Cell Framework]]). That fee is how the subsidiary funds whatever reserve it chooses to carry and anything it builds for itself. The subsidiary is a fractal of the office: it holds cash and assets of its own, and it holds its cells. So the office fee is split by who owns the growth. The office still charges one-third of the subsidiary's consolidated growth; the subsidiary's own books pay the part that grew outside the cells, and each cell pays the part that grew inside it. The costs cells share are still the cells' own, the space, taxes, and insurance a standalone subsidiary would pay for itself, so they move down to the cells that use them: cells that share a space lease it from the subsidiary, and costs like taxes and insurance divide by each cell's share of the pool. How thick a cushion to hold is the subsidiary's own call rather than a rule from above — the Leads set it together, and a thicker slice buys stability while a thinner one puts more in the cells. The check on running it too lean is that a subsidiary needing office capital to stay alive is a confidence question, not a funding request ([[The Governance Framework]]).

For a sense of the target shape, here is roughly how a healthy subsidiary's revenue divides:

| Line item                             | Target share of revenue |
| ------------------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Cost of goods                         | ~33.3%                  |
| Cost of fulfillment (the labor pool)  | ~13.3%                  |
| Cost of acquisition (outside spend)   | ~20%                    |
| Office fee                            | ~11.1%                  |
| Gain share                            | ~6.7%                   |
| Net margin                            | ~15.6%                  |

Every rule above is visible in those numbers. Cost of fulfillment is 20% of gross margin, and acquisition sits at 20% of revenue. Operating profit lands near 33.3%, and with nothing outside operations that's what book value grew by, so the office fee is one-third of it and the gain share is 20% of the same figure. Fixed expenses sit outside the table because they vary too widely between a repair shop and a factory to benchmark; in a live subsidiary they come out before operating profit, which scales the last three lines down together. These are benchmarks, not mandates.

## 2. The Office Pool

The office account is a war chest. Money pumps into it — fees, retained profit, returns — and nothing routinely comes out. Capital leaves only when Class A members decide to deploy it ([[Faleth Capital Constitution#Article IV Governance|Article IV of the Constitution]]). A bucket with no holes fills whether or not anyone is watching it.

The office runs one pool. Its own equity and any investor capital sit in the same place, and ownership is tracked as a percentage. If the office holds $2 million and an investor contributes $3 million, the pool is $5 million — the office owns 40%, the investor 60%. Every dollar is deployed by the same people under the same discipline, and the percentages decide who owns what share of the outcome.

Half that pool should be working and half should be waiting. **Deployed capital** is the combined book value of the operating subsidiaries; **liquid capital** is everything held in reserve. Keeping the two roughly equal is how the office covers the business and liquidity thirds of the one-third rule the whole system rests on ([[Faleth Capital Constitution#8.1 — The One-Third Rule and the Two Buckets|Article VIII of the Constitution]]).

The ratio drifts, and when it does it's a signal rather than a crisis. It works as a weight on every deployment: the further the pool leans toward deployed, the less the office commits, and the further it leans toward liquid, the more. Too much liquid means capacity — go find the next builder ready to launch. Each deployment is a Class A decision, and every one aims the same way: as close to half and half as the pool can get. An investor withdrawal comes off the liquid side, never out of an operating business, and if it tips the ratio, the weight shifts with it. Nothing is ever liquidated to satisfy a withdrawal.

## 3. The Quarterly Snapshot

Everything the office pays out is calculated once a quarter off a single number: **the growth of the total pool.** The pool is cash and assets together — liquid reserves plus the combined book value of every operating subsidiary underneath. Its growth is what that total is worth at the end of the quarter minus what it was worth at the start. If it rose, there's something to share. If it didn't, there isn't. Two balance sheets and a subtraction.

**The team's share comes off total pool growth.** Twenty percent of everything the pool gained goes to participants across every subsidiary, because their work is what made it grow. It comes off the total, every party's slice included, before anything is divided by ownership.

**Investors pay a 20% performance fee** on their share of what's left: the 20 of a fund's "2 and 20," without the 2, so there's no management fee. It's only charged on created value, measured against a **high-water mark**: an account pays only on growth that carries it past its previous high. The team's share and the performance fee are the only things that come off an investor's slice.

**Dividends are paid from cash, not from growth.** Most of what the pool gains is ownership: profit the subsidiaries kept. The cash it collects is mostly the fees. The office's share of those fees pays 30% to Class B shareholders as dividends, and the rest covers the office's modest operating costs and stays in the war chest. The office's share of the growth and the performance fee land in its equity. Retention isn't a target so much as the default: money that hasn't been explicitly paid out doesn't leave.

Both of those office-side numbers resolve to a figure per share. The dividend pool divides evenly across every Class B share outstanding, so the quarter's dividend per share is that pool over that count. What's retained raises the share itself: **book value per share** is the office's own equity divided by shares outstanding, which means every dollar the office keeps lifts the value of every share already held. Dividends reward holding; retention compounds it. Investor capital sits on the balance sheet as an asset and a matching liability that cancel out, so book value reflects only what the office itself owns.

Every one of these comes off the same snapshot, and every payment goes out in the same quarterly distribution — gain share and dividends landing together. The arithmetic is deliberately plain enough that anyone who wants to check it can, with a calculator and ten minutes.

## 4. The Two Gain Shares

Two gain shares pay out each quarter, and they exist to align two different scopes of performance.

**The subsidiary share** is 20% of the growth in a subsidiary's book value, distributed to the people who work there. It is drawn from the same number as the office fee and at the same time, so neither reduces the other's base. Weekly pay already rewards producing gross margin; this rewards running the whole operation well — controlling costs, holding quality, and earning the repeat business that shows up as profit rather than revenue.

**The office share** is the 20% of total pool growth from the previous section, distributed across every participant in every subsidiary. It gives someone at one subsidiary a real stake in whether the others are run well, since consolidated performance drives part of their own payout. Caring about the whole enterprise is structural here, not aspirational.

Both divide the same way: by your **Layer 2 income for the quarter**, as it appears on your pay. Total what the value share paid you over the quarter, divide by the total across everyone in that scope, and that's your fraction. The floor doesn't count toward it — Layer 1 is security, and gain share is a return on value created ([[The Contribution Framework#2. The Three Layers|The Contribution Framework]]).

In a multi-cell subsidiary the subsidiary share splits in half — half from the growth of the cell's own book value, paid to that cell's people, and half from the subsidiary's consolidated growth, which the subsidiary pays from its own books across every cell. The split arrives when a subsidiary goes from single-celled to multicellular, which is the point at which a cell's own performance becomes something its people can see and move ([[The Cell Framework#4. One Body, One Pool|The Cell Framework]]).

Both shares land in the same distribution, and at each one you choose independently: take it in cash, or mint it into Class B shares. Cash from one and equity from the other is a perfectly ordinary choice. The exception is a Class A member, who chooses between cash and a Managed Capital Account instead. How shares are minted, valued, and burned, and how that exception works, is in [[The Equity Framework#2. Earning and Cashing Out Class B|The Equity Framework]].

## 5. A Quarter at the Office

Numbers make this concrete. Say the pool holds $5 million: the office's own equity is $2 million and a single investor has $3 million in, so the office owns 40% and the investor 60%. There are 10,000 Class B shares outstanding, which puts book value at $200 a share. Over the quarter the subsidiaries pay $225,000 in office fees, and the pool grows by $500,000, including the fee cash that reached its liquid side.

| Step | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Office fees paid by the subsidiaries | $225,000 |
| Executive teams and mentors — 20% of the fees | −$45,000 |
| Fee cash to the pool's liquid side | $180,000 |
| Pool growth, fee cash included | $500,000 |
| Team's share — 20% of total growth | −$100,000 |
| Growth left to divide by ownership | $400,000 |
| → investor's 60% | $240,000 |
| → office's 40% | $160,000 |
| Performance fee — 20% of the investor's share | −$48,000 → office |
| **Investor keeps** | **$192,000** |
| Office total — its $160,000 plus the $48,000 fee | $208,000 |
| Office's 40% of the fee cash | $72,000 |
| → Class B dividends, 30% | $21,600 |
| → kept in the war chest | $50,400 |

What each party sees. **The executive teams and mentors** take their $45,000 before the fee reaches the pool. **The team** splits $100,000 across every participant in the portfolio by Layer 2 income, on top of whatever their own subsidiary's gain share paid; in this example they mint all of it. **Class B holders** divide $21,600 across 10,000 shares — $2.16 a share, so 500 shares pays $1,080. **Book value** rises as the office's $208,000 lands in its equity, less the dividend: $2,186,400 over 10,000 shares is $218.64, up from $200, a gain every holder keeps on top of the dividend. The team's $100,000 is then minted at that $218.64, about 457 new shares that start earning dividends next quarter. **The investor** nets $192,000 on $3 million, about 6.4% for the quarter. Had their account dropped $50,000 the quarter before, the performance fee would apply only to the $190,000 that carried it past its old high — $38,000 instead of $48,000 — while the team's share would apply as usual.

It reconciles: $500,000 of growth, less $21,600 paid in dividends, leaves the pool at $5,478,400 — the office's $2,286,400 plus the investor's $3,192,000. Had the whole team taken its share in cash instead, $100,000 would leave the pool, and the $180,000 of fee cash would still cover it and the dividends.

A flat or down quarter is much shorter to describe. No growth means no team share and no performance fee, since both are percentages of growth that didn't happen, and an investor pays nothing at all. Dividends come from the fees, so they shrink with the subsidiaries' growth and stop where it stops.

## 6. Why It Doesn't Break

Each layer solves its own problem — weekly pay for immediate productivity, the subsidiary share for local ownership, the office share for portfolio-wide thinking, dividends for long-term compounding. None was designed as a backup for the others. The resilience is a byproduct.

The deepest protection is that the largest expense flexes on its own. Labor is paid as a share of gross margin, so when revenue falls, labor cost falls with it, in the same proportion, without anyone deciding anything. A traditional company with fixed payroll bleeds whether or not customers show up, and its answer to a downturn is layoffs. Here the system contracts automatically and keeps its people. The floor is the only fixed labor cost, and it is deliberately modest ([[The Contribution Framework#2. The Three Layers|The Contribution Framework]]).

The layers also fail independently. Picture the office deploying hard into new subsidiaries that haven't matured: pool growth goes flat, so the office gain share pays nothing and performance fees stop, while dividends keep coming from the fees the mature subsidiaries send up. The operating subsidiaries don't notice. Their weekly pay still comes out of gross margin, their own gain share still calculates off their own growth, and participants minting shares are doing it while book value is temporarily suppressed — buying in at a discount that appreciates when those investments come good.

The system doesn't need every layer performing at once. It needs one, and the architecture makes it structurally difficult for all of them to fail together. For that to happen, every subsidiary would need to lose its customers, the office would need to have misallocated badly, and the reserves would have to be empty at the same time — while half the pool sits in liquid capital precisely so that last one isn't true.

## 7. Hours, Overtime, and Time on Task

The contribution model pays for value created, not time spent ([[The Contribution Framework]]). Until Faleth obtains a durable FLSA path that matches that design — opinion letter, exemption, or statute — operating subsidiaries that employ non-exempt participants still sit under federal wage-and-hour rules. This section is how we live with that gap without letting it rewrite the incentives.

**Hours are capped.** Soft warnings lead up to the limit; at **40 hours in a workweek**, systems refuse further task reservation until the next fiscal week. The point is cultural and economic: overtime's time-and-a-half premium rewards stretching hours, and Faleth wants the opposite — more value in less time, and room for a life outside work. A hard cap keeps groups from optimizing for duration the way they predictably would if OT were left open as upside.

**Time on Task (TOT)** is automatic: the clock runs from when work is reserved to when it is finished. No manual punch-in. That record exists for compliance and for a personal dashboard figure — rough **average dollars per hour** from Layer 2 over recorded task time — so people can see their own effectiveness. TOT is not the scoreboard. Points, calibration, and gain share are.

**Layer 3 ignores OT.** Both gain shares divide by Layer 2 value-share earnings only ([[#4. The Two Gain Shares|§4]]). Any overtime premium required by law is paid as a side effect of a time-wage regime that is not yet ready for this model; it does not inflate ownership stake or portfolio gain share. Floor (Layer 1) still does not count toward gain share.

**This is temporary posture, not the destination.** Class A members pursue DOL guidance and, longer term, statutory change so contribution-calibrated pay with participant-set hours can run without a time-wage overlay. Until that lands, the cap and TOT are how we comply without installing a management caste to police presence, and without making hustle the winning strategy.

Nothing here is legal advice. Exact classification, what counts as hours worked, and payroll mechanics are set with employment counsel for each subsidiary before offers go out.

*This is a living framework. The parameters — the office fee percentages, the gain-share rates, the dividend split, the deployment target — are set by Class A members through the amendment process in [[Faleth Capital Constitution#Article VII Amendments|Article VII of the Constitution]]. The principles are the parts meant to last: the war chest only fills, costs come off the top rather than pushing down, the biggest expense flexes with revenue, everything is calculated off one Faleth-book-value snapshot (realized economics only — unrealized gains excluded), and people are paid from the number they moved.*
