# The shortest version

**Government contracting is how a government buys things it can't make itself — under rules designed to prove it wasn't cheating.**

Not "procurement." Not "sales to the feds." The whole apparatus that exists because one party is spending *other people's money* and has to survive being questioned about it.

---

# Building it from zero

Imagine you are handed a budget that isn't yours, to buy something for thousands of people you'll never meet, and any one of them can haul you in front of a committee and ask why you picked that vendor.

You would do three things. You'd write down the rules *in advance*. You'd let everyone compete. And you'd keep a file proving you followed your own rules.

That's it. That's the entire genesis. Everything else — the FAR, sealed bidding, protests, cost audits, set-asides, GAO decisions — is a consequence of that one situation.

The asymmetry that explains everything:

| | Commercial buyer | Government buyer |
|---|---|---|
| Asks | "Will this make me money?" | "Will this get me fired?" |
| Spends | Their own money | Taxpayers' money |
| If wrong | Loses margin | Loses career, maybe testifies under oath |
| Rewards | Best product | **Best product I can defend** |

So the government isn't optimizing for the best deal. It's optimizing for the best deal *that holds up under adversarial review*. Those are different answers. That gap is the whole discipline.

And the gap runs through seven walls:

| # | Wall | The question | What removes it |
|---|------|--------------|-----------------|
| 1 | **Requirement** | "Is this needed, and is it actually funded?" | Budget line, validated need, color of money |
| 2 | **Notice** | "Does anyone know we want this?" | Market research, RFI, industry day, SAM.gov |
| 3 | **Eligibility** | "Are you even allowed to bid?" | Registration, clearance, certifications, responsibility |
| 4 | **Compliance** | "Did you follow every instruction?" | Reading the solicitation as a legal document |
| 5 | **Evaluation** | "Can we justify choosing you?" | Scored criteria, documented tradeoffs |
| 6 | **Survival** | "Will a loser get this overturned?" | Protest-proof record |
| 7 | **Delivery** | "Can you perform, and will you get paid?" | Execution, invoicing, audits |

Wall 7 feeds back into Wall 3: your performance rating becomes your eligibility next time. **The loop is the business.** Past performance is the only reputation that counts, and it's written by your customer into a government database.

# The one equation

$$\text{Award} = \text{Eligibility} \times \text{Compliance} \times \text{Technical} \times \frac{\text{Price}}{\text{Risk}}$$

Note the multiplication. **A zero anywhere collapses the whole thing.** A brilliant solution that missed one instruction in the proposal scores nothing. This is the single biggest difference from commercial selling, where a strong area can compensate for a weak one. In government contracting, it cannot.

Two moves only, same as any market: **raise technical/price attractiveness**, or **lower perceived risk** — of you failing, of the evaluator being second-guessed, of the contract blowing up. Most of the work is the second move.

---

# What it actually includes

- **Finding the money before the solicitation.** Requirements are shaped upstream, in budget cycles and market research. By the time it's posted publicly, the criteria are set and the incumbent usually has the advantage.
- **Choosing a vehicle.** Open competition, a pre-competed contract vehicle, a small-business set-aside, or a research agreement. The vehicle decides how many competitors you have and how long it takes.
- **Choosing a contract type.** Fixed-price makes *you* bear cost overruns; cost-reimbursement makes the *government* bear them. You're negotiating who owns uncertainty, and the choice tells the buyer how confident you are.
- **Being allowed to play.** Registrations, accounting systems, security requirements, certifications. Unglamorous, fatal if missing.
- **Writing a proposal that is a compliance document first and a sales document second.**
- **Performing and getting rated.** This is the marketing function. There is no advertising. There is a report card that the next buyer reads.

Advertising doesn't exist here. Neither does brand in the consumer sense, discounting, or "creating demand." Demand is set by appropriations, not by price.

---

# The two jobs

1. **Win the work.** Position early, be eligible, be compliant, be defensible.
2. **Keep the work.** Perform, get rated well, renew. Because the second job *is* the first job's pipeline.

Companies that are good at job 1 and bad at job 2 win once and die. The compounding asset is a track record, and it compounds slowly and against you if it goes bad.

# The math that keeps it honest

$$\text{Contract value} - \text{actual cost} - \text{cost of capital} = \text{profit}$$

That third term is the one commercial businesses forget. You typically deliver first and get paid later — so every contract is a loan *you* make to the government. Growth eats cash. A profitable contractor can go bankrupt expanding, because winning more work means financing more work.

---

# Three rules of thumb

- **Read the solicitation like a contract, because it is one.** Every "shall" and "must" is a pass/fail gate, not a suggestion. The bid/no-bid decision turns on compliance risk far more than on how good your solution is.
- **The real selling happens before the competition is announced.** By the time it's public, you are either positioned or you're a stalking horse. Upstream influence is worth more than a great proposal.
- **Cash flow and compliance kill more contractors than competitors do.** Underfunded growth and a broken accounting system end more companies than losing does.

<details>
<summary><strong>Where it gets more complicated (but not more fundamental)</strong></summary>

- **The rulebook** — the FAR and its agency supplements. Long, but it's all downstream of "prove you didn't cheat."
- **Vehicles** — schedules, government-wide contracts, indefinite-delivery contracts, blanket agreements. Pre-competed hunting licenses that shortcut Walls 3–6.
- **Set-asides** — reserved competitions for small, veteran-owned, women-owned, and distressed-area businesses. A genuine structural advantage if you qualify, and a ceiling on you as you grow.
- **Protests** — a losing bidder can challenge the award and, often, freeze performance. Wall 6 in the flesh. It's why decisions are documented to an almost absurd degree.
- **Cost accounting** — cost-based contracts come with accounting standards, audits, and disclosure requirements. The compliance burden scales with contract type.
- **Non-FAR agreements** — research and prototype agreements deliberately escape the rulebook to buy speed. You trade oversight for flexibility.
- **Conflicts of interest** — if you helped write the requirement, you may be barred from bidding on it. Advice has a price.
- **Teaming** — joint ventures and mentor arrangements let small firms borrow past performance and capacity they don't have. Often the only way past Wall 3.
- **The calendar** — money expires. Fiscal-year-end behavior, option periods, and funding cliffs drive timing in ways that look irrational until you know the rule.

</details>

---

**In one paragraph:** Government contracting is how a government converts tax money into goods and services while remaining defensible to auditors, competitors, and the public. The buyer isn't chasing the best deal — they're chasing the best defensible deal, which is why eligibility, compliance, and documented reasoning matter as much as quality and price. Supply is gated by rules, demand is set by budgets, and the only lasting reputation is a performance record the customer writes. Win the work, then earn the rating that wins the next one — and mind the cash while you do it.
