# Lyle — Distribution Before Production

## Context

Lyle connected Elon Musk's advice about sequencing ventures by capital intensity to the early Manscaped story. The example is user-supplied and should be independently verified before being cited publicly as corporate history.

## Raw reflection

> That story kind of reminds me of the story of, or that last point, or reminds me of the story of Manscaped. Because Manscaped originally started off as just a drop shipping company, but they just dropped ship just razors that were good for Manscaping, right? But the thing is that they actually started to do really well, and so they actually reinvested a lot of that into actually developing their own product line. And then over time, they just kept improving the product line, and now they're like a very well-known, very reputable brand.
>
> But I find it kind of funny because I've often said that business, you can really summarize basically, it's just the dynamics of supply and demand pretty much. It's just because the reason sales and marketing is so needed is because the supply doesn't know the demand, and the demand doesn't know the supply.
>
> And so if you can start off with that, you can just make money just by helping connect the supply to the demand. You can absolutely do that. That's what retail does. That's what so many businesses are based off of, so many sales marketing companies are based off of that. They're based off of things like distribution, and it takes no capital to really do distribution, it just takes effort.
>
> But once you start to make enough money, then you can actually move forward to actually creating the supply. And so that's one of the things I think is actually super fascinating.

## Continuation — The Law of Convenience

> I feel like another reason why starting off in sales and marketing is so important is because, like you mentioned on step two, learn through real transactions, that you learn who buys, why they buy, and what their pain points are. People primarily buy their pain, right? It is whatever they are looking for as the cure to their pain, essentially.
>
> I have also found that a lot of it is what I call the Law of Convenience. People pay you because you save them—usually you save them time more than anything else. It is not that somebody cannot learn how to do what you do. It is that the time investment required to do that does not make sense. It makes more sense to pay you so they do not have to learn the skill from the ground up.
>
> If you are a car mechanic and somebody else is a finance guy who is very good in his field, as long as the economics work, it makes more sense for him to work a little more in his field and pay you to fix his car. You already spent the time learning how to diagnose, debug, troubleshoot, replace parts, and use the tools. It is not that he cannot do it; it is far more convenient for you to do it, and he does something for you in return.
>
> I have found that is really the fundamental of business and the economy. Starting in sales and marketing gives you the ability to tap into that. Many businesses try to jump straight to the capital point, but they do not know their customer or the customer's pain points. Maybe they have a good product, but they do not know how to get it into anyone's hands. That is an opportunity for you to make money without putting down much capital—and to learn what you will need when you have the capital to start your own thing.

## Distilled claim

Distribution can serve as a capital-light apprenticeship for production: first connect existing supply to unserved demand through effort, then reinvest both margin and customer knowledge into proprietary supply.

The underlying value mechanism is the [[research/faleth/process/law-of-convenience-2026|Law of Convenience]]: customers buy when purchasing a specialist's result costs less than recreating the specialist's learning, tools, time, and risk themselves. Sales and marketing expose the entrepreneur to those real pain and convenience economics before heavy capital is committed.
