I asked ChatGPT what it thought the root causes of the baby boom was, and this was its response.

If you mean the post–World War II Baby Boom in the United States, roughly 1946 to 1964, it wasn’t caused by one thing. It was several forces hitting at once and reinforcing each other.
The biggest factors were:
Millions of servicemen came home at the same time. During the Depression and WWII, marriages and children had often been delayed. After 1945, a huge amount of that pent-up family formation happened almost simultaneously.
The economy suddenly looked much safer. The Great Depression had made people cautious about having children. After the war, the U.S. entered a long period of rising wages, high employment, enormous industrial capacity, and relatively strong confidence about the future.
Marriage happened younger and more often. The median age at first marriage fell substantially. People were getting married in their early 20s, which gave couples a longer window in which to have several children.
Housing became much easier to obtain. The GI Bill, federally backed mortgages, suburban construction, highways, and developments like Levittown made it possible for ordinary middle-class families to buy houses with room for children.
The GI Bill boosted household earning power. Veterans received help with college, vocational training, mortgages, and other benefits. That helped millions of men move into relatively well-paying careers much faster than they otherwise might have.
One-income households were more economically feasible. In many middle-class families, one wage could support a house, a car, and several children. That dramatically reduced the financial friction involved in expanding a family.
There was strong cultural pressure toward marriage and family life. After years of depression and war, domestic stability became almost an idealized national goal. Marriage, suburban homes, and children were presented as the normal trajectory of adulthood.
Contraception was less effective and less widely available. The birth-control pill did not become available in the U.S. until 1960. Family planning existed before then, of course, but couples had fewer reliable options for precisely controlling fertility.
Child mortality had fallen dramatically. Improvements in sanitation, antibiotics, vaccines, nutrition, and medical care meant that having several children no longer carried nearly the same likelihood of losing some of them that previous generations had faced.
One of the most interesting things is that the Boom wasn't simply "soldiers came home and everybody had babies." Birth rates remained unusually high for nearly two decades after the war ended.
That's because the country had essentially built an entire economic ecosystem around young families: good jobs → early marriage → inexpensive housing → children → suburban growth → schools and consumer spending → more jobs.
It became a self-reinforcing demographic flywheel.
And then, beginning in the late 1950s and especially the 1960s, many of those same conditions started changing: marriage ages rose, women's employment and educational opportunities expanded, contraception became much more effective, raising children became relatively more expensive, and cultural expectations shifted. That's why the Baby Boom eventually gave way to the Baby Bust.
