# *Maverick* — Employee Participation Versus Corporate Unilateralism

![[2026-07-12-maverick-semler-employee-participation.jpg]]

## Highlighted excerpt

> The sad truth is employees of modern corporations have little reason to feel satisfied, much less fulfilled. Companies do not have the time or the interest to listen to them, and lack the resources or the inclination to train them for advancement. These companies make a series of demands, for which they compensate employees with salaries that are often considered inadequate. Moreover, companies tend to be implacable in dismissing workers when they start to age or go through a temporary drop in performance, and send people into retirement earlier than they want, leaving them with the feeling they could have contributed much more had someone just asked.
>
> The era of using people as production tools is coming to an end. Participation is infinitely more complex to practice than conventional corporate unilateralism, just as democracy is much more cumbersome than dictatorship. But there will be few companies that can afford to ignore either of them.

— Ricardo Semler, *Maverick*

## Distillation

Semler identifies the central contradiction of conventional employment: executives describe people as the company’s greatest asset while preserving structures that deny those people meaningful voice, development, agency, and resilience during temporary weakness. The organization asks for commitment without constructing reciprocal participation.

His sharper point is that participatory governance is not advocated because it is administratively easier. It is **harder**—as democracy is harder than dictatorship—but it produces legitimacy, information flow, adaptability, and human commitment that unilateral control cannot manufacture.

## Faleth relevance

This is unusually direct external validation for Faleth’s founding premise: **the individual contributor is not a production input but a governing participant who bears consequences and therefore deserves voice, upside, and a legible path to stewardship.**

Faleth’s contribution pools, elected Leads, quarterly recalibration, earned equity, graduated sanctions, and removal-by-body architecture are attempts to make Semler’s moral argument operational rather than decorative. Participation cannot mean an annual survey followed by management doing whatever it had already prepared in PowerPoint. It must include actual authority over working conditions, leadership, economics, and conflict resolution.

The necessary caution is equally important: participation increases coordination cost. Faleth therefore needs bounded domains, clear interfaces, transparent metrics, and explicit decision rights so democracy does not decay into meeting saturation or political theater.
