The people doing the work and bearing its consequences should govern the conditions under which they work.

### Rationale

Traditional management imposes oversight from above. In this model, governance exists only to handle what the incentive structure cannot manage automatically — collective decisions, dispute resolution, and leadership selection. The core belief is that the people closest to the work know best how to do it, and those who share the financial consequences of decisions are the right people to make them.

Governance is kept lightweight so it does not become a political or power struggle. The governing rule is: **keep each decision with the people closest to it; widen the circle only as the scope of impact grows.** Trust is the default for local, visible, reversible action; transparency replaces prior permission; and standards define outcomes without fossilizing every isolated mistake into permanent law.

Ricardo Semler supplies an important constraint: participation is not superior because it is simpler. It is “infinitely more complex” than corporate unilateralism in the same way democracy is more cumbersome than dictatorship. Its justification is that people are participants rather than production tools—and that organizations gain legitimacy, information, adaptability, and commitment by treating them accordingly. The design problem is therefore not to eliminate the cost of participation, but to bound it through clear decision rights, transparent economics, small governing cells, and explicit interfaces.

### Examples from Practice

- Subsidiaries and cells elect their own Leads.
- Quarterly recalibration processes allow teams to adjust parameters they live under.
- Disputes are resolved through defined mechanisms rather than top-down fiat.
- Office-level decisions (Class A voting) are reserved for those who have earned stewardship through contribution.
- As subsidiaries scale, they divide into self-organizing cells rather than adding management layers. Each cell runs its own contribution pool with clear input/output interfaces to other cells.
- A cell’s practical scale limit is **human legibility**: once participants cannot understand what is happening, influence important decisions, know one another’s contributions, debate strategy, or experience belonging, the unit should prepare to divide.
- Semco’s participatory model treats employee voice and development as structural authority rather than executive rhetoric; the complexity is accepted and bounded rather than used as an excuse for unilateral control.
- Leadership is representative, elected, and removable; authority exists to coordinate and unblock, not to set the economics of the people beneath it.
- Governance protects interfaces and shared risks while leaving local methods free to evolve.
- Repeated disagreement can trigger a parallel trial or cell split rather than forcing one faction into permanent submission.
