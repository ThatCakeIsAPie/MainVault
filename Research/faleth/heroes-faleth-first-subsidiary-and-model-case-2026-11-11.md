# Heroes + Faleth, First Subsidiary, and Why the Model Works

Lyle's notes, Nov 11, 2026. Canon is the nine docs in `Business/Faleth Capital/`. If anything here conflicts with them, canon wins until it is amended.

## How Heroes and Faleth fit
- These two are my life's work. They stay separate for compliance, and they were designed to work together.
- Heroes develops the people. Faleth is the vehicle capable people build with.
- Amway gives equity in the people you develop. Faleth gives equity in the work you do, in any industry. They are sequential, not the same mechanism.
- Shared philosophy: give more than we take, and anyone can leave whenever they want.

## First subsidiary plan
- I build the first subsidiary myself and earn Class A the same way anyone would: eight straight profitable quarters. Then people I developed build the next ones and I become their mentor, not their CEO.
- Seed money comes from the defense business my brother and I are partners in. That business is not part of Faleth.
- First business: a small electronics contract manufacturer. About $100k for used equipment, leased space, and setup, plus about $100k for parts and early pay. Roughly $250k to start, my own money, no loan.

## The economics
- Labor is a fixed percentage of gross margin, so most costs are known before the first unit ships. The only costs that aren't a percentage are amortized overhead (lease, power, insurance).
- So the game is not "how cheaply can we do this." It's "how many units do we need to break even," and everything past that is margin.
- Industry margin figures come from shops paying hourly labor, so they don't describe this model.
- A dollar saved is a dollar earned: cheaper parts raise gross margin on every ticket, and the people who found the saving see it in their pay.
- No idle payroll to cover, so quotes don't need padding for slow periods.

## Why it wins even in the pessimistic case
1. Fewer managers: same units, fewer people, so output per head rises with no behavior change.
2. Retention: it takes about a year for someone to become really profitable. A shop that keeps its people stops paying that ramp cost over and over.
3. Learning curve: repetition makes people faster without rushing. The points stay the same while the time shrinks.
4. Extra effort from aligned incentives is upside, not something the model depends on.

Fear causes more botched work than ambition does, because people rush to protect a job. Everything compounds: skill, process improvement and retention.

## Core arguments
- A hire is a bet; a partner is a split. We don't make bets, we compound. Opposite of the VC model (99 fail, 1 pays for all).
- Investor story: no exit, no multiple, book value in and out, returns from businesses getting better over decades.
- A wage is a fixed payment regardless of output, the same flaw people criticize in welfare and planned economies, running inside a company. Managers exist to patch the motivation the wage removed.
- An employee's client is the boss, not the customer. The real transaction is buying time and attention. Paying from the ticket makes the customer the worker's client too.
- The floor is a draw against future earnings, the most common thing in business. It removes survival anxiety and should be something you forget is there. Only a new participant's floor is a grant.
- Unfilled work prices itself: if nobody picks up a process, raise its points until someone does.
- The honest case for wages: they're cheap to administer because presence is easy to verify. The answer: every serious shop already tracks the work for customers and auditors. Pay from the data, and the data fills itself in and stays accurate.
- Lesson from my first repair shop job: data entry has to be how the job gets done, not an extra chore.
- The model forces you to build a business that runs without you, which is what every owner wants. The wage model builds the owner a prison.
- "It pays people who care to care."
- Universal because every industry already has a ticket: a unit of work that gets invoiced. The model needs only a unit of work and a record of who touched it. Industries differ in the mix of direct and indirect processes, which is calibration.
- Roles aren't abolished, assignment is. Someone can be the best welder in the shop for twenty years because they chose to.

## Draft clause: Training override
Status: draft. Intended for Contribution Framework §4, after the Lead paragraph. Not yet added to the framework file.

> **Training.** Skill should outlive the person who holds it, so teaching is a pointed process. When someone agrees to train another person on a process, the trainer earns an override: a share of the points the trainee earns on that process, set by the team at calibration, with one-third as the starting guideline. The trainee keeps their full points; the override is added to the ticket, not taken from them, so a job in training carries slightly more points than the same job done alone. That is the cost of building a second pair of hands, and it is paid by the whole pool because the whole pool benefits when the work no longer waits on one person.
>
> An override runs on the same clock as a new participant's floor: the remainder of the quarter in which it begins and the whole of the next. At the calibration that follows, it expires on its own unless the team votes to renew it. Silence removes it. A renewal is a decision that the training is still happening, not a courtesy to the trainer, and a trainee who has been working on their own for months is the evidence that it isn't. The same mechanism serves a specialist who wants to step back from the bench: train enough people, and the override is the bridge from doing the work to having made it possible.

Design choices:
- The override is additive, so the trainee doesn't pay for their own training.
- One-third is a guideline the team can change.
- It expires by default, so the burden is on keeping points on the board.
