# Texas Mini-Triangle Asset Accumulation Thesis

## Thesis

Lyle’s long-term Faleth plan includes accumulating productive assets inside the Texas economic triangle: **Dallas–Fort Worth ↔ Austin–San Antonio ↔ Houston–Galveston**, including the connecting corridors and intermediate nodes such as Waco, Temple–Killeen, and College Station.

The bet is not merely that “Texas grows.” It is that advanced manufacturing, logistics, energy, defense, technology, and population growth will increasingly behave as one connected regional system. Land and operating assets bought before that integration is fully priced may gain both strategic utility and value over decades.

## Why this geography fits Faleth

- **Home-field advantage:** Lyle already lives in DFW and can build local knowledge outward rather than speculate in a remote market.
- **Multiple demand engines:** The region is not dependent on one employer, city, or industry.
- **Corridor optionality:** Intermediate markets can benefit from logistics, housing, utilities, industrial expansion, and reduced effective travel times between metros.
- **Faleth compatibility:** Land can host or support future operating companies, warehousing, light industrial activity, energy infrastructure, housing, or other productive uses. The asset can therefore create utility before appreciation is realized.
- **Long horizon:** The thesis is measured in decades, allowing infrastructure and industrial clustering to compound without requiring perfect short-term timing.

## Acquisition principle

**Buy utility with appreciation optionality—not appreciation stories with carrying costs.**

Cheap land is not automatically undervalued land. A parcel should ideally possess at least one defensible present-day use or path to cash flow while preserving exposure to regional growth.

## Underwriting gates

Before treating a parcel as an asset rather than an attractively photographed liability, test:

1. **Access:** Legal road access, freight access, and realistic travel time to a growing node.
2. **Water:** Existing service, groundwater rights and quality, or a credible extension path.
3. **Power and connectivity:** Available electrical capacity, broadband, and upgrade economics.
4. **Flood and environmental risk:** FEMA maps, drainage, wetlands, contamination, and insurability.
5. **Entitlements:** Zoning, deed restrictions, ETJ exposure, annexation risk, and permitted uses.
6. **Tax and carry:** Property tax, agricultural valuation eligibility, maintenance, security, and insurance.
7. **Demand catalyst:** Existing employers, population, logistics flows, or funded infrastructure—not only a concept map.
8. **Exit depth:** More than one plausible future buyer or operator.
9. **Productive use:** Agriculture, storage, industrial yard, housing, energy, operating-company site, or another way to offset carry.
10. **Price discipline:** Compare against local income, rents, replacement cost, comparable acreage, and infrastructure cost rather than assuming every cheap acre converges to metro pricing.

## 2026 price baseline

Texas A&M's verified-transaction data through Q1 2026 puts the broad rural regions intersecting the mini-triangle at **$8,028/acre for Austin–Waco–Hill Country**, **$8,960/acre for Northeast Texas**, and **$11,698/acre for Gulf Coast–Brazos Bottom**. These are moving regional medians for large rural tracts, not parcel-specific appraisals.

Current undeveloped-land asking medians are higher and more local: Limestone **$9,928/acre**, Freestone **$11,050**, Robertson **$11,498**, Falls **$11,868**, Navarro **$12,500**, Milam **$15,000**, Madison **$16,503**, and Burleson **$18,279**. Asking prices are not closed-sale prices, and small subdivided tracts usually carry a substantial per-acre premium.

The initial acquisition search band should therefore treat roughly **$8,000–$12,000 per acre** as the present central-interior baseline for larger, genuinely rural tracts. Pricing of **$15,000–$18,000+ per acre** indicates that Austin/College Station spillover, tract subdivision, utilities, frontage, or development expectations are already being capitalized.

### Forty-year scenario frame

At a $10,000/acre basis, forty years of nominal appreciation would produce approximately **$32,620/acre at 3%**, **$70,400 at 5%**, or **$149,745 at 7%** annual growth. These are sensitivity cases, not forecasts. The real return must be measured after inflation, property tax, maintenance, financing, transaction costs, and any income produced by the land.

## Geographic research frame

Track the triangle as a network of nodes rather than buying indiscriminately inside a giant outline:

- DFW southern/eastern approaches
- Waco and the I-35 spine
- Temple–Belton–Killeen
- Austin–San Antonio corridor and its outer rings
- College Station/Bryan and east–west connections
- Houston’s western/northwestern approaches
- Houston–Galveston industrial/logistics corridor

The highest-upside locations may be where two systems overlap: highway plus rail, manufacturing plus power, population growth plus constrained developable land, or logistics access plus comparatively low basis.

## Sequencing

1. **Now:** Build the map, watchlist, parcel criteria, and price history; do not let cheap land distract from VXE cash landing.
2. **First acquisitions:** Favor small, understandable assets with low carry and real present use.
3. **Expansion:** Use operating-company cash flow and local knowledge to accumulate adjacent or strategically linked assets.
4. **Portfolio phase:** Hold a mix of land, industrial/operating real estate, and infrastructure-adjacent assets across multiple nodes rather than one speculative concentration.

## Evidence and uncertainty

The X post’s “mini-triangle” transport network is a proposal, not a verified funded buildout. Terafab is a high-signal industrial ambition, but a promotional render does not underwrite surrounding acreage. This thesis should therefore be validated through actual project sites, permits, utility investment, employer commitments, population flows, transaction prices, and funded transportation work.
