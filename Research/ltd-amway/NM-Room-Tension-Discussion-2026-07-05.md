# Network Marketing Room Tension — Discussion Summary

## Context

A shift in the local network marketing room's culture prompted this reflection: leadership has moved toward tighter information control (discouraging talk of members' success outside the environment) and a longer, higher-touch prospect intake process (five 1-on-1s plus three weekly meetings before a decision, up from an original quick screening interview). Both changes are philosophically defended by leadership but sit uneasily against Lyle's own model of the business and his broader Faleth Capital vision.

## Thread One: Information Control ("Don't Discuss Outside Success")

**Leadership's stated reasoning:** Prospects who hear about someone else winning in an unrelated venture (e.g., a large real estate profit) get distracted mid-training and chase the shinier opportunity instead of building the skill in front of them. This is a real, defensible concern about protecting early-stage attention.

**Clarified scope (key finding):** The restriction, on reflection, extends beyond competing income opportunities — it also mutes discussion of personal growth in domains that aren't monetized (health, relationships, confidence, character). This is the sharper problem.

**Lyle's core thesis:** Network marketing's actual product is personal development, not the physical products sold. Money flows because people switch buying habits, and the thing that earns that switch — absent product superiority — is relational trust built through visible growth in the person doing the selling. If that's true, visible growth stories aren't a distraction from the business model; they *are* the top of the funnel. A rule that broadly suppresses growth stories may be working against the exact mechanism that makes the model work, even though its narrower goal (don't let people chase competing ventures) is legitimate.

**Open question, not yet resolved:** Whether early-stage distractibility is a fixed trait present at intake (leadership's implicit assumption) or something that fades as conviction builds — which would mean the suppression should be time-bound to new people rather than applied indefinitely and broadly.

## Thread Two: The Extended Interview Funnel

**Current process:** Five 1-on-1s and three weekly meetings before a decision on a prospect, up from an original quick screening interview + single meeting invite. Leadership's justification: a longer filter produces higher-quality people, and people value what they had to work to get into (~ "spoon-feeding you with a fire hose"). The meetings function less as sales and more as relationship-building and early training — so people arrive at "yes" already partly prepared to run.

**Lyle's structural concern:** Network marketing's growth math depends on compounding invitation velocity — the more people inviting simply and often, the faster the numbers double. A long funnel raises the cost-per-prospect and caps how many people any given leader can process, which throttles the exponent the entire model depends on. This is a real bottleneck regardless of the quality argument's merit.

**Unresolved empirical question:** Whether the longer funnel has actually hurt intake volume and, more importantly, 90-day retention, since a slower funnel could theoretically out-compound a faster one if it produces people who stay and build for years rather than quit early. This hasn't been tracked or checked — it's currently a live disagreement between two plausible models, not a settled fact either way.

## Thread Three: Personal Fit and the Ministry-vs-Business Question

Lyle's leadership style is low-narration, high-demonstration: show what he's doing, let actions speak, answer questions when they arise, invite when someone seems aligned — rather than a scripted, front-loaded pitch process. This naturally conflicts with a heavily gated, information-managed environment.

Historically, Lyle suppressed his own bigger ambitions to fit the room's expectations during an earlier, more people-pleasing phase. He is at peace with his ambitions now, but friction with the room's current posture still surfaces the underlying question: **is this environment a business or a ministry, and does its process reflect trust in people as adults or management of them?** This is the same standard Lyle has already written into the Faleth Capital Constitution (Section 1.5, Section 1.8, Article VI) — voluntary participation, radical trust, earned rather than assigned hierarchy, an open exit.

**Resolution reached:** Direct confrontation with the room isn't the right move. Lyle's stated strategy — build his own results, avoid contesting policy directly, earn influence over time — mirrors the Constitution's own philosophy of letting proof-of-concept subsidiaries make the case rather than argument.

## The Dojo Vision (Emerging Resolution)

Lyle's vision for how he wants to run his own team, layered inside the existing org: treat the NM room as a **mastermind and skills dojo** for everything he's building, not a closed identity.

- A **simple standard for entry** to his team/inner circle (not the org's long interview funnel) — gating who's in, not managing what people already inside are exposed to.
- Once in, members are free to talk about and pursue other ventures. Recruit? Go on an invitation spree. Sell? Go try it. Win elsewhere? Go do it. The room is where people return to because it's valuable, not because information is withheld elsewhere.
- Money made through the room feeds Bucket One (per the Constitution's Two Buckets philosophy).
- Deeper layers of commitment are earned through **qualifier-based events** (business volume/structure thresholds) — conviction built through demonstrated results, not manufactured through a longer screening gauntlet. This mirrors the Constitution's principle of earned, re-earned hierarchy (Section 1.5) rather than the interview-based model.
- This directly operationalizes the Five-Step Blueprint's Step 1 ("start in network marketing... the foundational education") in Lyle's own terms.

## Current Operational Reality (Important Clarification)

Lyle is **still bound to his upline's full intake funnel** for actual prospect processing. The "simple standard for entry" described above is the vision he is building toward — not yet a live, operating system. This means:

- The compounding-math bottleneck (Thread Two) is still fully in effect for Lyle right now. The dojo reframe doesn't fix the funnel-length problem; it changes the meaning and culture around it, not the mechanics.
- What Lyle likely does control **today**, independent of the funnel: how he personally mentors people once they're on his team, how open he is about growth stories within his own circle, how much he shows vs. tells, and how convicted he builds people post-intake.

This produces **two distinct projects, not one**:

1. **Live now, within current constraints:** Shaping everything downstream of intake — mentorship style, openness about growth, culture within his own team. This can generate his own proof of concept without contesting the upline's process.
2. **Future, dependent on leverage:** Building enough influence in the room (or enough independent structure) to actually implement a different front door / intake standard. This is the "earn influence as I grow" path — real, but on a longer timeline than Project One.

**Caution flagged:** Don't let the aspirational vision (Project Two) substitute psychologically for acknowledging the live cost the current funnel is imposing on growth velocity (Project One's constraint). The reframe should coexist with, not replace, honest tracking of that cost.

## Open Threads for Future Discussion

- Whether to informally test/track retention and volume differences between the current long funnel and a hypothetical shorter one, to move the Thread Two disagreement from competing intuitions to evidence.
- How and when to introduce more of the dojo framing or Faleth Capital philosophy to upline leadership (Len or others) — organically, low-pressure, as previously discussed elsewhere.
- Continued discernment on the ministry-vs-business framing as the room's culture evolves, using the test: is friction coming from "they're wrong about growth mechanics" (an operational disagreement) or "this doesn't feel like being trusted as an adult" (a values violation) — since only the latter touches something the Constitution treats as non-negotiable.
